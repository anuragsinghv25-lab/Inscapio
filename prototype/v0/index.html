Cities are warming. In the demo model used on this site, Madrid’s year in 2070 starts to resemble a different city’s: summers are hotter, winters are milder, and the gap between the seasons grows.

Climatologists describe this with classifications built from temperature and rainfall. A place keeps its label until the pattern of its year moves far enough to earn another.

    What is InScapio?

    The web is full of things worth knowing.

    Most of them arrive the same way: a headline, a paragraph, another paragraph, a scroll.

    What if an idea could be explored?

    What if understanding meant moving, choosing, comparing and going deeper only when you want to?

    That is what InScapio is trying out: a small set of experiences, built to find out whether ideas land better when you can step inside them.

    Every experience has a deeper layer. It stays closed until you ask for it.

    This is what a deeper layer looks like: the context, the sources and the harder questions that would crowd a first pass. Open it where you are curious. Skip it where you are not.

    A climate is a pattern, not a day.

    Scientists sort climates with a few questions about a place’s year:

    How hot?The warmest month
    How cold?The coldest month
    How dry?How much rain, and when

    Answer those and a city gets a label. When the answers shift, the label can too, which is why a warming world changes where a city “belongs”.

    This follows the idea of the Köppen classification, drawn up in the early 1900s. This demo uses a simplified rule set with one rough dryness flag per city, so labels are approximate.

      Analogue matching: we compare the city’s projected yearly mean and seasonal swing with every other city’s present values, and pick the nearest. Weighted: mean temperature counts double the seasonal swing.

      Weather changes daily. Climate moves slowly, then all at once.

      Whose Body Is It?

      A clear-eyed book on safety, honour, gender and respect. For the girl walking home, the boy who is learning what "no" means, the parent who is worried, and the elder who grew up in a different time.

      Read it simply. Tap "Deeper" at the top whenever you want the root causes, the research and the hard arguments.

      Chapter 1 · Why this book

      A phone buzzes. A family goes quiet.

      Every few months a story shakes the country. A girl and a boy coming home from coaching are stopped, surrounded and humiliated while strangers film it. We feel anger. Then the arguments begin, and the arguments often hurt as much as the news.

      "Why was she there?" "Girls should be careful." "Hang them all." "Not all men." "It's the village mindset." "It's feminism's fault." Everyone shouts, nobody listens, and the girl, who did nothing wrong, is forgotten.

      This book slows the shouting down. It does not ask you to pick a team. It asks you to hold several true things at once, and to be brave enough to say all of them.

      A person can be given advice to reduce a risk without giving the person who harms them an excuse.

      Most online debates fail because each side hears a different claim. One person says "gender norms shape violence" and the other hears "all men are guilty." One says "be careful" and the other hears "it was your fault." Separating risk from responsibility, pattern from person, and motive from conduct removes most of the heat.

      Chapter 2 · The one idea

      Your body is yours. Nobody else's.

      Everything in this book grows from one sentence. No person's body becomes another person's property because of sex, clothes, marriage, drink, caste, family name or a community's idea of honour.

      For a child: If something feels wrong, you may say no, even to a grown-up, and you will not be in trouble for telling.
      For a teenager: "No" is a full sentence. So is silence, fear, or being too drunk to answer. Only a clear, free "yes" is a yes.
      For an adult: Marriage, love, a date, a favour or a job does not cancel this. Consent can be taken back at any moment.
      Respecting this is not weakness. It is what strength looks like.
      Chapter 3 · Root causes

      Violence does not appear from nowhere

      Think of a tree. The harm is the fruit we see on the news. But roots run deep. Tap each layer to see what feeds it.

      Two warnings. A risk factor is not a prediction: most people who grow up around violence never harm anyone. And not every offender is driven by hatred of women. Some are driven by power, impulse, a group's approval or the belief that they will never be caught.

      Public-health bodies such as the CDC and WHO list the same themes again and again: hostility toward women, acceptance of violence, rigid ideas of masculinity, peer groups that reward sexual aggression, alcohol and drugs, and weak community or institutional penalties. The better wording is: sexual violence can be gendered without being reducible to hatred of women.

      Chapter 4 · Moral policing

      How "honour" becomes control

      It almost always starts with a sentence that sounds like care: "Her behaviour reflects on our family." Tap each step and watch how far it travels.

      The dangerous shift: a woman stops being a person with her own life and becomes "a daughter, a sister, a wife, a symbol", and the community starts to claim her choices.

      A fair line: a parent may dislike a grown child's choices. Disapproval is not abuse. It becomes abuse when it turns into threats, confinement, humiliation, forced obedience, violence or "punishment".

      The asymmetry at the heart of it: a man's behaviour is treated as his own business. A woman's behaviour is treated as the family's and the community's business.

      Backlash. When old arrangements are challenged, some people feel their status, identity or family order is under threat. That explains the reaction. It never excuses violence.

      Reverence is not freedom. A culture can worship women as mothers, goddesses and keepers of honour while limiting their movement, inheritance, sexuality and decisions. Ideals and everyday practice can pull apart. Naming that gap does not mean the tradition is bad. It means we ask how the ideal reaches real life.

      Chapter 5 · "Dehati soch"

      It is not about villages. It is about control.

      Many women know this moment: you live independently in a freer setting, then you enter a conservative community and are quietly judged "characterless" for your clothes, your male friends, your late nights, a drink or a cigarette.

      The observation is real. But "rural people are like this" is too wide, and unfair to millions of kind villagers. The sharper idea is conservative, honour-based gender policing. It lives in villages, in cities, in rich homes, in offices with degrees on the wall and in every religion.

      Why villages can feel more intense: everyone knows everyone, reputation is currency, families depend on each other and gossip travels fast. Close ties also bring real support. The same network can protect and watch at once.
      Modern outside, old inside: someone can use the latest phone, work for a multinational and live in a metro while holding old beliefs about women's sexuality. Economic modernisation and attitude modernisation are different things.
      Question the belief, not the whole place or people it comes from.

      The Jamui case (as reported in September 2026). News reports describe an incident on 19 September in which a teenage girl and a male classmate, returning from coaching around 6 to 7 in the evening, were stopped and surrounded by a group, with assault, filming and circulation of the video. A case was registered under BNS and POCSO provisions, an SIT was formed and arrests were reported. What it can show: how "I disapprove" slides into "I can stop you" and "I can control you". What it cannot show: that the whole village thinks this way, or what each accused person's motive was. That is for investigators. Please never share the victim's identity or video. Also beware: unrelated old clips were falsely passed off online as arrest footage. Facts can change as the case develops, so check current reports.

      Chapter 6 · Risk or blame?

      "Why was she out at night?"

      Safety advice and blame can use almost the same words. The difference is who ends up carrying the guilt.

      The laptop test. Leaving a laptop on a car seat raises the chance of theft. It does not make the thief the owner. Reducing risk and assigning responsibility are two separate questions.
      The clock test. If the same judgment is made at 2 a.m., 10 p.m., 7 p.m., 5 p.m., at work, at home and inside marriage, it is about respectability, not safety.

      Try it. Is this fair safety advice, or victim-blaming?

      ClaimWhat can be trueWhat does not follow
      "The area was risky."The risk can be real.The victim caused the crime.
      "She could have taken another route."Precautions can reduce exposure.She consented to harm.
      "She knew men can be dangerous."Awareness informs choices.The offender got permission.
      "Her clothes attracted attention."People notice clothes.Attention equals consent.
      "She should be more careful."Caution can help.Responsibility moves to her.

      Also remember: most sexual violence is not a stranger in a dark alley. It is usually someone the victim knows, such as a partner, an acquaintance or a person in authority. With children, "why did she go there?" is never an adequate question.

      Chapter 8 · Punishment and culture

      Does fear change a society?

      Many say "hang them" in the first hour of anger. That anger is understandable. But we should look at what works.

      Research on deterrence, summarised by the US National Institute of Justice, finds that the certainty of getting caught matters more than how harsh the penalty is. Offenders often believe they will escape, act on impulse, are drunk, or are driven by anger and a wish to dominate. None of them are doing a careful sum about sentences.

      Does that mean punishment is pointless? No. Punishment gives accountability, keeps dangerous people away from others and tells everyone that this harm is serious. It is just not enough alone.

      "I won't, because I fear prison" is an outside fence. "I won't, because another person's body is not mine to override" is an inner fence. A society needs both, and the second one lasts.

      WHO's RESPECT Women framework (2025) names seven strategies: Relationship skills, Empowerment of women, Services, Poverty reduction, safer Environments, Child and adolescent abuse prevention, and Transformed attitudes, beliefs and norms. Notice that law is only one part of a bigger picture.

      Chapter 9 · Feminism, fairly

      What to accept. What you may challenge.

      Feminism is argued over fiercely, so let us be fair to both its supporters and its critics.

      Accept: equal worth, equal rights, and bodily autonomy for all. A woman is not property, and her "no" is final.
      Equality is not sameness. Equal worth does not require pretending that everyone has identical bodies, tastes or roles.
      Freedom is not rebellion. Choosing a traditional life can be free. Rejecting it can be free. The question is always: was it her own choice?
      Empowerment is not exemption. Equal rights come with equal responsibility for harmful conduct.
      Feminism is not one voice. Feminists disagree about family, religion, sex, work, motherhood and masculinity.
      You may criticise: selective empathy, hatred of men, ideological overreach, or treating every disagreement as evil. You can say this and still believe in women's equality.
      Handle "patriarchy" with care. It names systems of unequal authority well. It should not be a magic word that explains every difference between men and women or every individual act.
      Support equal rights and bodily autonomy, and stay free to criticise anyone, however righteous their cause, who behaves unfairly.
      Chapter 10 · Boys, men and fairness

      Men are not the enemy. Men are half of the answer.

      If this book only warned women, it would be half a book. Boys need a picture of strength that does not need anyone to submit.

      Healthy masculinity: strength includes self-control. Confidence includes handling rejection. Protection does not mean ownership. Leadership does not mean one-man authority. Sexual success never means overcoming someone's resistance.
      Courage, duty and protectiveness are not anti-woman. They turn harmful only when mixed with entitlement or domination.
      Men can be victims. Bodily autonomy is universal, so male survivors deserve belief and care too. This does not erase the gendered patterns in the data, and the data does not erase male victims.
      False allegations exist, and so does due process. A fair system investigates carefully, protects the accused and punishes proven lies. But some false reports do not show most reports are false. Survivors should be heard. The accused should get a fair hearing. Both can be true.

      Do not turn these points into weapons. "Men can be victims" should widen protection, not be used to dismiss women. "Women are more often victims" should not be used to dismiss men.

      Chapter 12 · What you can do

      Prevention belongs to everyone

      Teaching girls safety and teaching boys consent are not rivals. One lowers exposure, the other lowers harm done, and strong institutions lower impunity.

      The ideal end: the law does not need to stand in every room, because every person already carries the boundary inside.

      If you or someone you know needs help (India)

      112 emergency police · 181 women's helpline · 1098 Childline · 1930 cyber-crime helpline (also cybercrime.gov.in for reporting online abuse or leaked videos). If you are in danger, call now. If you are a survivor: it was not your fault, and you deserve support.
